The content pipeline

How we turn a question into a film, a Short, a Hindi version or an interactive experience, all made in code, with no camera and no editing timeline. Pick what you're making, follow the steps, and track each production at the bottom of this page.

The idea in one paragraph

A film starts as a text script with a short label on every sentence. A voice model reads each sentence, and the program notes the exact second each one starts. That list of times is the film's clock. Everything else follows the clock: the animation is code that draws each frame from "which sentence is playing", the music puts its big moments on chosen sentences, and the subtitles come straight from the script. Change a sentence and everything moves with it. Claude (the AI assistant) does the research, writing and code. You stay the editor: you approve the story and the look before anything is built, and you watch the film before it goes out.

What are you making?

Every format uses the same seven-stage process (step 2). What changes is the story shape, the extra ingredients and the checks. Pick one to see its recipe.

Explainer film: "How does this actually work?"

Start from a question a normal person asks, break it down to basics, and let the viewer discover the idea. Technical words come only after the idea has landed.

Length
9–15 min
Examples
How ChatGPT Actually Works · Images From Noise · Money vs a WhatsApp Message
Approvals
story, then look

What makes it work

  • Everyday examples (a cup of hot… chai, Sachin Tendulkar, a viva exam).
  • A device that runs through the film, and one moment where the idea is shown, not said.
  • Where it can, the explanation is a recognisable machine, with a window into the part doing the work.
  • Every number on screen comes from one small model, so the numbers never disagree.
  • Optional: a tiny model trained for the film, so it can show real outputs (as in Images From Noise).

Done when

  • The critic can restate the message from the frames alone.
  • Every fact in the script has its source in the description.
  • Film, chapter files, Shorts, thumbnails and the upload kit are ready.
  • The master is on the masters branch.

Evidence film (In Context): "Why is this happening?"

Not news. Several headlines that look separate, the force that connects them, the system explained from basics, then back to the headlines, now making sense. Calm, fair, sceptical; the viewer decides.

Length
12–18 min
Example
The AI Race Is Becoming an Electricity Race
Approvals
dossier + evidence + script, before any build

Extra ingredients and rules

  • Real evidence only: real report pages with the quoted line highlighted; headlines as clean citation cards (headline, outlet, date). No fake newspapers or AI imagery posing as evidence.
  • Every claim has a class: measured fact, official projection, company announcement, estimate, analysis, or contested. The narration says which ("IEA projects…").
  • Announcements say their stage: announced, under construction, operating.
  • The counterargument gets its own section, with the music stopped.
  • "Information as of [date]" on screen and in the description, plus a corrections log.

Done when

  • You approved the research dossier, the evidence wall, the fact table and the script.
  • Every on-screen claim shows its source and date.
  • The counterargument is fair enough that a critic of the thesis would accept it.
  • Film, Shorts, thumbnails, upload kit with sources and the corrections note are ready.
Kick it off in Claude Code
/make-film An In Context film: "[the headlines or question]". Information as of [date]. First build the research dossier, an evidence wall of real page captures, a fact table with a claim class for every claim, and the script. Stop for my review before any scene code.

Business film (Business, Unpacked): "What's the machine underneath?"

How a remarkable business actually works: the customer's problem, the product, the technology, why customers pay, how money flows, the suppliers, the flywheel, the moat, the risks, and what it would take to build it again. Not a company profile, not stock tips.

Length
15–20 min
Example
Why Can Only One Company Build This Machine? (ASML)
Approvals
dossier + script, then look

Extra ingredients and rules

  • The Business Engine diagram, built on screen in every episode.
  • "What could break the engine?" gets its own section, with the music stripped back. Never "unstoppable".
  • On screen and in the description: "This film explains a business. It is not investment advice."
  • Numbers only when they reveal structure, each with "as of [quarter]".
  • Code-built machines are labelled "conceptual illustration". No fake logos.

Done when

  • The Business Engine is complete and the red-team section is honest.
  • The not-investment-advice note is on screen and in the description.
  • Film, Shorts, thumbnails and upload kit are ready.
Kick it off in Claude Code

Imagine (Past or Future): "What would it actually look and feel like?"

A world we can't visit, built from evidence plus careful imagination, always labelled apart. Past reconstructs (Kurukshetra, through one ordinary soldier). Future is evidence-based time travel: one ordinary day in one plausible future (India 2040).

Length
13–20 min
Examples
Kurukshetra · India 2040
Approvals
dossier + design, then look

Extra ingredients and rules

  • Painted worlds drawn in code: streets, homes, people, light that changes through the day.
  • Labels everywhere: every imagined frame says IMAGINED; every real insert shows its source and date.
  • Future: the loop (freeze, rewind to today's evidence, fast-forward back), "one India that could exist", never "will", and three possible futures at the end.
  • Past: what the texts say, what is interpretation, what is invented, kept distinct. Real archive film for any "then" beats that were filmed.
  • No clichés: flying cars, neon cities, holograms, fake Devanagari.

Done when

  • Every frame is clearly imagined or clearly evidence.
  • Each future element answers: what must become true, who builds and pays, what can go wrong.
  • Film, Shorts, thumbnails and upload kit are ready.

Shorts: one idea, vertical, under a minute

Cut from a finished film. Chosen sentences are re-rendered in a 1080×1920 frame with a big title on top and large subtitles below, and each Short gets its own music that builds to its key line.

Length
15–60 s each
How many
3–7 per film
Examples
every film's shorts/ folder

Rules

  • One idea per Short. A hook in the first 1–2 seconds.
  • Rearranged for vertical, never just cropped. Text stays in the middle of the frame, away from the app's buttons.
  • It must make sense with the sound off.
  • End by pointing to the full film.
python3 videos/<film>/shorts.py

Done when

  • Each Short was checked on its own stills, not only the film's.
  • Each has a title, a description and a link to the full film.

Hindi version: the same film in another language

A second script and voice, with every word on screen written in both languages, so one switch renders the whole film in Hindi. Hindi sentences run about 30% longer, so each one stores a stretch factor and the animation inside it slows to match.

Voice
Kokoro hm_psi at speed 0.9
Example
ChatGPT Asal Mein Kaise Kaam Karta Hai (17:38)
Extra check
Whisper pronunciation test

Steps

  1. Write narration_hi.txt with the same labels as the English script.
  2. Write every on-screen word as tr('English', 'हिंदी').
  3. Record and time it against the English timeline, then render with the Hindi switch on.
  4. Run the Whisper listening test, and respell any word it mishears.

Done when

  • No English left on screen (checked on contact sheets).
  • Pronunciation error rate is low on every line, and the misheard words were fixed.
  • Hindi thumbnails, upload kit (youtube_hi.md) and Hindi Shorts are ready.
BUILD_DIR=build_hi NARRATION=narration_hi.txt TTS_LANG=hi VOICE=hm_psi \
  SPEED=0.9 REF_TIMELINE=build/timeline.json python3 engine/audio/tts.py videos/<film>
BUILD_DIR=build_hi FP_LANG=hi scripts/render_all.sh videos/<film>

Lab experience: a world you enter, take apart and break

The interactive home of the channel at firstprinciplesstudio.in. The visitor is an actor, not a passenger: predict, act, see the consequence, ask why. Built in a separate repository (FirstPrinciplesInteractive) with Next.js and 3D.

Live
Inside AI · HUMAN · India 2040 · Inside ASML · Kurukshetra
Modes
Story · Explore · Map · Break it · Why? · Evidence
Approvals
plan, then expert review

Rules

  • A meaningful action every 20–45 seconds: one that changes what the visitor understands.
  • Direct manipulation beats buttons: drag the mirror into the beam, turn the valve.
  • One model of the system drives every view, so story, explore and map always agree.
  • Every claim carries a trust label; a Map mode works without 3D.
  • Narration uses the same channel voice.

Done when

  • The interaction audit passes and the tests are green.
  • It works on a phone and without WebGL (Map mode).
  • The sources and trust labels are complete, and the expert-review status is shown.
  • It's deployed and linked from the matching film.
npm run dev                 # http://localhost:3000
npm run typecheck && npm test
npm run audit:interaction   # fails on passive stretches > 45 s
npm run build:static        # → out/ for Hostinger

Step 2

How every film is made: seven stages

One Claude agent plays seven roles, one after another. Each role has its own job, writes its own file, and passes a check before the next one starts. This procedure is saved as a Claude Code skill: type /make-film in the studio repository. Everything a stage decides is written to videos/<film>/review/, so work can pick up again in a new session.

1director

Story: decide what the film says

  • The core message in one sentence. If it needs two, it's two films.
  • Chapters and beats: for each beat, what the viewer now knows. A hook in the first two seconds.
  • Research from primary sources; every number gets its source and date. Missing evidence is found or asked for, never invented.
  • A first script, sized to the length: our films run 108–134 spoken words per minute.
✓ You approve the story
review/APPROACH_REVIEW.mdreview/RESEARCH_DOSSIER.mdnarration.txt
2art director

Look: decide how it looks, moves and sounds

  • Colours with meanings, fonts with roles, the frame grid and safe areas.
  • The motion language: one easing style, a basic timing unit, how scenes hand over to each other.
  • The storyboard: per scene, its composition, its one main technique and how it hands over to the next.
  • Where every real image comes from (page captures, licensed photos, archive film), and what this film must avoid.
✓ You approve the look
review/PRODUCTION_DESIGN.md
3animator

Build: voice, clock, animation, music

  • Script with labels. Each sentence has a short label like [c]; a scene starts with @; ~1.6 is the pause after a line; {…} is how to say a hard word.
  • Voice. Kokoro (free, open, runs on a normal computer) reads each line into its own file, so changing one sentence re-records only that sentence. The channel narrator is a 65/35 blend of a Hindi and an American voice.
  • Clock. The program measures each clip and writes when every line starts. That file drives everything else.
  • Animation. Each scene is a function that answers "what does the screen look like at time t?", drawn 30 times a second. Scenes ask "when does line c start?", never "at 61.8 s", so they stay in sync when the script changes.
  • Music and sound. The "Organ & Time" score is written in code from sound waves, with its big moments placed on chosen lines. Under speech, only the music's voice frequencies dip.
  • Subtitles come straight from the script and the clock, so no speech recognition is needed.
@ game lead=0.4 tail=0.6
- [b] "A cup of hot..." ~1.6        # 1.6 s of silence: the viewer guesses
- [c] You probably said tea. ~0.4

// the answer pops in exactly when the narrator says line [c]
const pop = S.at('c', 0, 0.6, E.outBack);

4render engineer

Preview: prove it before rendering it all

  • Check that the clock exists, and that every font, asset and archive clip loads with no scene errors.
  • Render still frames and a contact sheet per scene, then a short preview of the hardest part.
  • The full render (about 35 minutes for a 17-minute film on 4 CPU cores) happens only after the critic passes the preview.
node engine/video/render.mjs --video videos/<film> --contact <scene> --n 9 --out sheet.png
node engine/video/render.mjs --video videos/<film> --scene <scene> --at 4.5 --out still.png
5critic

Review: judge the pixels, with fresh eyes

  • A separate agent that didn't build the film looks only at the approved plan and the rendered frames, never at the builder's description.
  • It scores the hook, clarity, composition, phone-size readability, motion, timing, continuity, honesty labels and sound sync, from 1 to 10.
  • It reports the three biggest problems, each with its exact frame, what's wrong, why it matters, the smallest fix, and what must not change.
  • Last test: it tells you the film's message from the frames alone. If it can't, the story fails.
review/FINDINGS.md
6repair

Fix: small, local, bounded

  • Fix only what the critic found, keep everything that was approved, and re-render only the affected frames.
  • At most two tries per problem. If it still fails, stop and ask you.
  • Never weaken a check or a label to make the review pass.
  • Pass means every score is 8 or more, no open problem, and the message is restated correctly.
7delivery

Deliver: film, Shorts, thumbnails, upload kit

  • The full film, plus web-sized chapter files that join back together losslessly.
  • Shorts, rearranged for vertical and reviewed on their own stills.
  • Thumbnails drawn with the film's kit, 2–3 versions for YouTube's Test & compare.
  • The upload kit: titles, a description with every source and archive clip, chapters from the clock, tags, a pinned comment.
  • The full-quality master goes on the masters branch, split into 95 MB pieces with join scripts.
  • Then you watch it and decide when it's published. A machine check can't judge tone.
scripts/render_all.sh   videos/<film>                # picture, 4 workers in parallel
scripts/finalize.sh     videos/<film> <Name>         # picture + sound → out/<Name>.mp4
scripts/split_parts.sh  videos/<film> <Name>         # chapter files in film/
node videos/<film>/thumbnail.mjs                      # thumbnails
scripts/publish_master.sh videos/<film> <Name>       # master → masters branch

Ingredients and where they come from

Everything runs on one ordinary computer (4 CPU cores, no graphics card), and all of it is free and open source, apart from the AI assistant.

Archive film, in short. Not everything on archive.org is free to use: only about 1,900 of the 10,500 Prelinger films carry a public-domain dedication, and the 78rpm collection is for research only. In the US, recordings become public domain 100 years after they were published, so only those from 1925 or earlier qualify. So every item gets a verdict before use: green (free to use), amber (only after a written check) or red (only with a licence). Only the seconds needed are downloaded, the original soundtrack is removed, and every shot is credited with its year.
Step 4

Quality rules for every piece

Code makes a film possible; craft makes it worth watching. These come from our own films and from the open-source guides we reviewed (see the ideas log).

Story

One message per film, one new thing to know per scene. A hook in the first two seconds. Where possible, the explanation is a recognisable machine with a window into the working part.

Time for the viewer

One thing to take in at a time. Text stays up at least 1.2 seconds per 8 words. Fast actions, held meanings.

Motion

Nothing moves in a straight line at constant speed. Only one thing that matters moves at a time. Characters anticipate, overlap and never move in unison.

Picture

One focal point, real empty space, text inside the safe area. Vertical versions are rearranged, never cropped.

Sound

Every visual event has a sound and every sound has a reason. Not every cut gets a whoosh. Loudness at −14 LUFS.

Honesty

Every fact shows its source; invented numbers say "illustrative"; imagined frames say "imagined"; archive film shows its year. Every number in a scene comes from one model, and sped-up time carries a badge (×4).
