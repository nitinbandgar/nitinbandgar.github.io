The idea in one paragraph

A film starts as a text script with a short label on every sentence. A voice model reads each sentence, and the program notes the exact second each one starts. That list of times is the film's clock. Everything else follows the clock: the animation is code that draws each frame from "which sentence is playing", the music puts its big moments on chosen sentences, and the subtitles come straight from the script. Change a sentence and everything moves with it. Claude (the AI assistant) does the research, writing and code. You stay the editor: you approve the story and the look before anything is built, and you watch the film before it goes out.

What are you making?

Every format uses the same seven-stage process (step 2). What changes is the story shape, the extra ingredients and the checks. Pick one to see its recipe.

Explainer film: "How does this actually work?"

Start from a question a normal person asks, break it down to basics, and let the viewer discover the idea. Technical words come only after the idea has landed.

Length
9–15 min
Examples
How ChatGPT Actually Works · Images From Noise · Money vs a WhatsApp Message
Approvals
story, then look

What makes it work

  • Everyday examples (a cup of hot… chai, Sachin Tendulkar, a viva exam).
  • A device that runs through the film, and one moment where the idea is shown, not said.
  • Where it can, the explanation is a recognisable machine, with a window into the part doing the work.
  • Every number on screen comes from one small model, so the numbers never disagree.
  • Optional: a tiny model trained for the film, so it can show real outputs (as in Images From Noise).

Done when

  • The critic can restate the message from the frames alone.
  • Every fact in the script has its source in the description.
  • Film, chapter files, Shorts, thumbnails and the upload kit are ready.
  • The master is on the masters branch.

Evidence film (In Context): "Why is this happening?"

Not news. Several headlines that look separate, the force that connects them, the system explained from basics, then back to the headlines, now making sense. Calm, fair, sceptical; the viewer decides.

Length
12–18 min
Example
The AI Race Is Becoming an Electricity Race
Approvals
dossier + evidence + script, before any build

Extra ingredients and rules

  • Real evidence only: real report pages with the quoted line highlighted; headlines as clean citation cards (headline, outlet, date). No fake newspapers or AI imagery posing as evidence.
  • Every claim has a class: measured fact, official projection, company announcement, estimate, analysis, or contested. The narration says which ("IEA projects…").
  • Announcements say their stage: announced, under construction, operating.
  • The counterargument gets its own section, with the music stopped.
  • "Information as of [date]" on screen and in the description, plus a corrections log.

Done when

  • You approved the research dossier, the evidence wall, the fact table and the script.
  • Every on-screen claim shows its source and date.
  • The counterargument is fair enough that a critic of the thesis would accept it.
  • Film, Shorts, thumbnails, upload kit with sources and the corrections note are ready.
Kick it off in Claude Code

News explainer (In Context, 60–90 s): "Why does this headline reach me?"

One headline, traced to the viewer's own life in 60 to 90 seconds, paced like a thriller. Evidence first, then the chain of consequences, then the one number that matters.

Length
60–90 s
Example
The Strait and the Field (pilot)
Approvals
script + fact table, then the look + asset plan

The toolkit

  • Highlighter citation cards: the real headline, outlet, date and a verbatim excerpt; the key words swept in yellow (red for danger) as the narrator says them.
  • Real clippings: the outlet's own page, captured by Claude or uploaded by you, laid on screen as a paper cut-out.
  • A world map with routes: India's official boundary, with routes and ships flowing; labelled "illustrative".
  • A lens transition: a circle opens out of one picture into the next idea (the strait opens into a field).
  • A paper-collage flow: halftone cut-outs (plant, farmer, government), with goods and money travelling between them.
  • A chart that lands on the number, with an estimate drawn dashed.
  • Edit: hard cuts on the hits; the last frame held as the poster.
  • Sound: the channel narrator and a thriller score scored to the words (a relentless pulse, a rising bass, silence before the turn).
  • 2–4 AI shots at most, for the hook, scale or the human moment, always labelled (step 4).

Done when

  • Every figure is in the fact table with its source and claim class.
  • No AI shot stands in for evidence, and every one is labelled.
  • Whisper understands every word over the score.
  • −14 LUFS, true peak at or below −1 dBTP.
  • "Information as of [date]" is on screen and in the description.
  • A Short and a thumbnail are cut from it.

Imagine (Past or Future): "What would it actually look and feel like?"

A world we can't visit, built from evidence plus careful imagination, always labelled apart. Past reconstructs (Kurukshetra, through one ordinary soldier). Future is evidence-based time travel: one ordinary day in one plausible future (India 2040).

Length
13–20 min
Examples
Kurukshetra · India 2040
Approvals
dossier + design, then look

Extra ingredients and rules

  • Painted worlds drawn in code: streets, homes, people, light that changes through the day.
  • Labels everywhere: every imagined frame says IMAGINED; every real insert shows its source and date.
  • Future: the loop (freeze, rewind to today's evidence, fast-forward back), "one India that could exist", never "will", and three possible futures at the end.
  • Past: what the texts say, what is interpretation, what is invented, kept distinct. Real archive film for any "then" beats that were filmed.
  • No clichés: flying cars, neon cities, holograms, fake Devanagari.

Done when

  • Every frame is clearly imagined or clearly evidence.
  • Each future element answers: what must become true, who builds and pays, what can go wrong.
  • Film, Shorts, thumbnails and upload kit are ready.

Shorts: one idea, vertical, under a minute

Cut from a finished film. Chosen sentences are re-rendered in a 1080×1920 frame with a big title on top and large subtitles below, and each Short gets its own music that builds to its key line.

Length
15–60 s each
How many
3–7 per film
Examples
every film's shorts/ folder

Rules

  • One idea per Short. A hook in the first 1–2 seconds.
  • Rearranged for vertical, never just cropped. Text stays in the middle of the frame, away from the app's buttons.
  • It must make sense with the sound off.
  • End by pointing to the full film.
  • A shape that works for "what if" Shorts: the event lands on its word → pull back to show the scale → dive in to make it personal ("you") → the consequence, with a live counter → an escalation → a light moment → a callback, then the title.
python3 videos/<film>/shorts.py

Done when

  • Each Short was checked on its own stills, not only the film's.
  • Each has a title, a description and a link to the full film.

Hindi version: the same film in another language

A second script and voice, with every word on screen written in both languages, so one switch renders the whole film in Hindi. Hindi sentences run about 30% longer, so each one stores a stretch factor and the animation inside it slows to match.

Voice
Kokoro hm_psi at speed 0.9
Example
ChatGPT Asal Mein Kaise Kaam Karta Hai (17:38)
Extra check
Whisper pronunciation test

Steps

  1. Write narration_hi.txt with the same labels as the English script.
  2. Write every on-screen word as tr('English', 'हिंदी').
  3. Record and time it against the English timeline, then render with the Hindi switch on.
  4. Run the Whisper listening test, and respell any word it mishears.

Done when

  • No English left on screen (checked on contact sheets).
  • Pronunciation error rate is low on every line, and the misheard words were fixed.
  • Hindi thumbnails, upload kit (youtube_hi.md) and Hindi Shorts are ready.

1director

Story: decide what the film says

  • One message, in one sentence. If it needs two, it's two films. Then chapters and beats: what the viewer knows after each one.
  • The hook: movement in the first 15 frames, a question in the first 2 seconds. Real stakes from the real world (a headline, a number, a person) beat a definition.
  • Research from primary sources (official alerts, filings, papers, the company's own page), then a fact table: every number with its source, date and claim class (measured · official projection · company statement · estimate · analysis · contested). Missing evidence is found or asked for, never invented, and widely repeated figures are traced to their origin (the Morris worm's "6,000 machines" turned out to be an extrapolation).
  • The script, sized to the length (108–134 spoken words a minute for explainers, up to 165 for Shorts). Jargon only after the intuition. If picture and voice don't fit, tighten the words; never pad the voice.
  • The beat sheet: every line names the one thing that changes on screen and the word that triggers it.

2art director

Look and asset plan: how it looks, moves, sounds, and what it's made of

  • Look: colours with meanings, fonts with roles, the grid and safe areas, an avoid list (no hooded hackers, no code rain, no stock clichés).
  • Motion: one easing family; small things snap, panels settle, the camera glides; things that appear pop on a spring; text rises into place; scenes hand over by design (a hard cut on a hit, a match, a lens), not a fade everywhere.
  • The storyboard: per scene, its composition, its one main technique and how it hands over to the next.
  • The score's story: one theme that changes with the film (introduced, transformed, resolved or left hanging), the cue sheet, and the tempo the narration will lock to.
  • The asset plan (step 4): beat by beat, code, archive, a real upload or AI, with the reason, where to find it, and for AI a full prompt card.

·assets

Assets: collect, generate, check

  • Claude finds:
    • captures of public web pages (official alerts, reports, articles) as clippings;
    • public-domain archive film from archive.org;
    • openly licensed images.
  • You supply: small files uploaded to the film's assets/inbox/; large footage as a Google Drive or Dropbox link. Claude reviews it shot by shot and proposes the seconds to use.
  • AI shots: generated from Claude's prompt cards (still first, then the clip), by you or by Claude with your approval.
  • Every file is checked (size, length, AI tells, the right era), credited and recorded. Anything without a credit and rights note, or marked test-only, can't be released.

python3 scripts/assets.py ingest videos/<film>                 # checks and records every upload
3animator

Build: voice, clock, animation, music

  • Script with labels.
    • Each sentence has a short label like [c], and a scene starts with @.
    • ~1.6 is the pause after a line, and {…} is how to say a hard word.
    • {clip:KEY} plays a real person's own voice (a soundbite) at the narrator's loudness, with its own subtitle.
  • Voice. The channel narrator (Kokoro, a 65/35 blend of a Hindi and an American voice) reads each line into its own file, so changing a sentence re-records only that sentence. Pronunciations are checked with Whisper, and fixed with respellings where it mishears.
  • Clock. Every line starts on the score's beat, so voice and music move together. Whisper also gives the time of every word, so animation and music hits land on the word.
  • Animation.
    • Each scene answers "what does the screen look like at time t?", 30 times a second.
    • Scenes ask "when does line c start?" or "when is 'cracked' said?", never "at 61.8 s".
    • Optional motion blur at render.
  • Music. A score written in code with its own story: a theme, a driving pulse for tension, real silence before turns, hits on words. It sits 10–14 LU under the voice and is pulled down further in the speech frequencies whenever anyone talks.
  • Subtitles come straight from the script and the clock.

Preview: prove it before rendering it all, then measure it

  • Check the clock, fonts, assets and archive clips load with no scene errors. A missing or unlicensed asset stops the render.
  • Render stills and a contact sheet per scene, then a short preview of the hardest part.

5critic

Review: judge the pixels, with fresh eyes

  • Independent: a separate agent that didn't build the film sees only the approved plan and the rendered frames, never the builder's description.
  • Scores, 1 to 10:
    • the hook (and the last frame as a poster);
    • clarity, composition and readability at phone size;
    • motion, timing and continuity;
    • honesty labels and sound sync.
  • Assets: nothing AI stands in for evidence, every AI shot is labelled, every real one credited, and no AI tells are visible at full size.
  • Findings: the three biggest problems, each with its exact frame, what's wrong, why it matters, the smallest fix, and what must not change.
  • Last test: it restates the film's message from the frames alone. If it can't, the story fails.
review/FINDINGS.md
6repair

Fix: small, local, bounded

  • Fix only what the critic found, keep everything that was approved, re-render only the affected frames.
  • Fix in this order: missing information and unreadable text first, then timing, then visual detail.
  • At most two tries per problem. If it still fails, stop and ask you. Never weaken a check or a label to pass.

Deliver: film, Shorts, thumbnails, upload kit

  • The film, plus web-sized chapter files that join back losslessly.
  • Shorts: rearranged for vertical (never cropped), each with its own hook and reviewed on its own stills.
  • Thumbnails: 2–3 versions for YouTube's Test & compare. One idea each: a face or an object, three words at most, readable at phone size.
  • The upload kit:
    • titles;

  • Your viewing: you watch and listen on headphones and a phone speaker, then decide. A machine can't judge tone.

Archive film, in short. Not everything on archive.org is free to use: only about 1,900 of the 10,500 Prelinger films carry a public-domain dedication, and the 78rpm collection is for research only. In the US, recordings become public domain 100 years after they were published, so only those from 1925 or earlier qualify. So every item gets a verdict before use: green (free to use), amber (only after a written check) or red (only with a licence). Only the seconds needed are downloaded, the original soundtrack is removed, and every shot is credited with its year.
Step 4

Real footage, clippings and AI shots

Most of a film is drawn in code. Some beats are stronger with something from outside: a real newspaper clipping, real footage, a photograph, or an AI-generated image or clip. For every film, Claude writes an asset plan with the look. It says, beat by beat, what to use, why, where to find it, and for AI shots the exact prompt. You approve it, then generate or collect the assets and upload them. Full rules: docs/ASSETS.md.

Where AI helps most

The hook (one cinematic frame in the first 2 seconds), scale (a supertanker in a narrow strait), the human turn (one farmer, one field), transitions (a field dissolving into dots on the map) and imagined worlds. Keep AI to about 20% of the runtime, 3–5 seconds a shot: 2–4 shots in a 90-second explainer, 6–12 in a long film.

Where it doesn't

Anything a viewer might take as proof, anything code can show exactly, and any shot that only fills time. Real material has no cap, only a reason.

AI material: two ways to make it

You make the stills (the default)

Claude writes a prompt card for every AI shot in the asset plan. You run each one in the image tool you like, make 2–3 variants, and upload them to the film's Drive folder in AI stills/, named by key. Use any tool whose terms allow commercial use on YouTube.

Claude makes the motion

Claude picks the best variant (or asks for another try) and moves it in code: a slow push, drift and light effects, at no cost. Where real motion matters, such as a blink or a hand, it uses image to video in Runway (connected to this workspace) or Higgsfield. It quotes the credit cost first and starts only after you approve.

AI material: a prompt that looks real

Generate the still first, choose it, then turn that still into a clip (image-to-video), so the clip keeps the composition. Make 3–4 variants and keep the best. Each line of the prompt has a job:

  • Shot and lens: these words make it look photographed rather than rendered.
  • Palette: ties the shot to the film's colours, so the cut doesn't jump.
  • Frame: leaves room for the text we add in code.
  • Avoid: removes the usual AI tells.
Example prompt card: the hook of the Hormuz pilot
STILL
Shot     wide establishing shot · 35 mm lens · low drone, 30 m above the water
Subject  a fully laden crude-oil tanker, dark hull riding low, moving through a narrow sea lane
Place    a narrow strait at dusk: arid rocky headlands in haze behind
Light    ten minutes after sunset, cool blue water, a thin amber band on the horizon, deck lights on
Palette  deep navy and slate with one amber accent
Frame    ship on the left third, bow to the right; empty sky upper right for text; horizon on the lower third
Realism  photorealistic documentary photograph, natural colour, subtle 35 mm grain, no HDR, no lens flare
Format   16:9, 3840×2160
Avoid    text, letters, numbers, logos, flags, names on the hull, watermarks, people, warships, CGI look
CLIP (image-to-video from the chosen still)
Camera   slow push-in, about 3%, perfectly steady
Motion   only the tanker gliding left to right, a soft wake, small waves, drifting haze
Length   5 s at 24–30 fps
Avoid    morphing, new objects appearing, horizon tilting, camera shake, any text
LABEL    AI-generated illustration

Spec

Stills: at least 1920×1080, ideally 3840×2160 so the slow push stays sharp. Clips: at least 1920×1080, 24–30 fps, 5 s, with no audio, watermark or subtitles. Shorts need their own 9:16 version.

Check before uploading

Hands and faces. No letters or numbers anywhere. Physics: the wake, reflections and shadows. Scale. The right era, uniform, flag or vehicle. No flicker or morphing in a clip. Regenerate rather than fix, and keep the tool, prompt and seed.

How to upload

  1. Each film's asset plan (videos/<film>/ASSETS.md) gives every asset a key, e.g. tanker_dusk.
  2. Name the file by its key: tanker_dusk.mp4, bs_headline.png.
  3. On GitHub, open FirstPrinciplesStudio → videos/<film>/assets/inbox/ → Add file → Upload files → Commit. Browser uploads take files up to 25 MB, so trim clips to the seconds you need first.
  4. If anything differs from the plan, add tanker_dusk.txt with lines such as tool: …, prompt: …, seed: … (AI), or credit: PIB, 1 Feb 2026 and rights: … (real).
  5. Tell Claude. It runs scripts/assets.py ingest, which:
    • converts clips to 1080p30 and removes the sound;
    • records size, length and a checksum;
    • flags anything missing or too small.
    Claude then looks at every asset, and asks for a new version with a corrected prompt if it shows AI tells.

Large footage (over 25 MB): share a link instead

  1. Put the original files in Google Drive (a file or a whole folder) or Dropbox, set sharing to "Anyone with the link can view", and send Claude the link. Don't trim or re-export: originals are best.
  2. Claude downloads them and reviews everything:
    • every shot, with in/out timecodes and three frames on contact sheets;
    • flags for dark, blown-out, soft and shaky shots;
    • a timecoded transcript of any speech.
  3. Claude proposes the selects: which seconds go where, and why. You approve them.
  4. Only those seconds are cut, at high quality, and kept with the film, each recorded with its source file and timecodes. The raw files stay in your Drive; nothing large goes into the repository.

Soundbites: a real voice instead of the narrator

A clip's own sound can take over from the narrator for a line, for example a minister's sentence or a news anchor's warning. It plays at the narrator's loudness and gets its own subtitle, and the picture and music time themselves to it like any other line. News channels' soundbites stay in private tests unless they're licensed or cleared as a short quotation.

On screen, honesty is automatic. Real uploads show their credit, AI shots show "AI-generated illustration", and archive film shows its year. Clips play in sync with the narration. Photos get a slow push. Clippings land as paper cut-outs with the highlighter sweeping the lines the narrator reads. A missing asset, or an upload without its credit and rights, stops the render; a clipping can't be AI. Until an asset arrives, the scene runs in code, so nothing waits on it.

Step 5

Quality rules for every piece

Code makes a film possible; craft makes it worth watching. These come from our own films and from the open-source guides we reviewed (see the ideas log).

Story

One message per film, one new thing to know per scene. A hook in the first two seconds. Where possible, the explanation is a recognisable machine with a window into the working part.

Time for the viewer

One thing to take in at a time. Text stays up at least 1.2 seconds per 8 words, counted from when it becomes readable; read it aloud to check. Lay out every text for its longest version, including the Hindi one. Fast actions, held meanings.

Motion

Nothing moves in a straight line at constant speed. Each kind of object has its own motion: small controls snap, panels settle, the camera glides almost unseen. Only one thing that matters moves at a time. Characters anticipate, overlap and never move in unison. Things that appear pop on a spring (a small overshoot, then settle); text rises into place rather than fading in whole.

Edit

Scene changes are designed: a hard cut on a hit, a match, a push or a lens, not a cross-fade everywhere. The last frame is held as the poster frame. Text and on-screen moments land on the exact word the narrator says. Before building, a beat sheet names, for every line, the one thing that changes on screen and the word that triggers it. If the picture and the voice don't fit, tighten the words; never pad the voice.

Picture

One focal point, real empty space, text inside the safe area. Vertical versions are rearranged, never cropped.

Sound

Every visual event has a sound and every sound has a reason. Not every cut gets a whoosh. Listen with your eyes closed: the soundtrack should have a shape of its own. Sound effects land 2–3 frames before the picture. Loudness at −14 LUFS with true peak at or below −1 dBTP; check it on headphones and a phone speaker.

Score like a thriller

A thriller needs a machine running under it, not a soft bed: a relentless pulse of short string notes, a pumping bass, nervous high strings, drums that build into rolls, and risers into every hit. On top, the tricks of Dunkirk, Inception, Interstellar and The Dark Knight: one small motif, a ticking clock that lands exactly on the hit, one low note that only grows, a heavy "braam" on the three or four biggest moments, dead silence before the turn, an unresolved ending. Sit it close under the voice (too far under and it sounds like a documentary), keep it out of the voice's frequencies, and land every hit on its word.

Outside assets

Evidence is real or rebuilt in code, never AI. Real people appear only in real photos. AI shots are labelled on screen and kept to about 20% of the runtime. Every upload carries its credit and rights, or it won't render (step 4).

Honesty

Every fact shows its source; invented numbers say "illustrative"; imagined frames say "imagined"; archive film shows its year. Every number in a scene comes from one model, and sped-up time carries a badge (×4).

Release checklist

  • ☐ Story, look and asset plan approved by you.
  • ☐ review/QA.md reads ALL PASS, with no test-only assets in the cut.
  • ☐ Expert review recorded (you, for technical films).
  • ☐ Critic pass: every score ≥ 8, no open finding, message restated.
  • ☐ You watched and listened on headphones and a phone speaker.
  • ☐ Description: every source with its date, every asset credit, "information as of", a corrections line; AI disclosure set in YouTube Studio if AI shots are used.
  • ☐ 2–3 thumbnails in Test & compare; Shorts reviewed on their own stills.
  • ☐ Uploaded as private first: YouTube's copyright Checks show no claim, or every claimed clip is replaced.
  • ☐ Master on the masters branch; production tracker updated.
  • ☐ Reminders set to review analytics at 7 and 28 days.
